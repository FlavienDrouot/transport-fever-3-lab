How to read crossover charts

Phase focus expands the leadership phases and gives the final phase only 30% of the space of an earlier phase. Both axes stay continuous, with real distances and times at the ticks; their scale changes at the crossover boundaries. Curves are clipped to the displayed window. The window ends shortly after the last leader change; crossings between slower trains do not extend it.

With multiple leadership phases, orange grid lines and double marks show changes in a Phase focus scale. They can introduce bends in curves, independently of train acceleration. A single phase has no scale changes. Choose Linear on both axes for uniform scales.

    Model & inspiration

    THE MODEL

    From traction to power, then a speed limit.

    At low speed, tractive effort limits acceleration. As speed rises, available power becomes the limit. The train then reaches its vehicle or track speed cap, or approaches a lower equilibrium speed when resistance balances power.

    a(v) = min(2 × Fmax, P / v) / m − 0.02 − 0.38 × g sin(θ)

    F is catalogue traction in N, P is power in W, m is empty mass in kg, v is speed in m/s, g = 9.80665 m/s², and θ = atan(gradient / 100). The 0.38 grade factor is fitted to two MAN road runs and provisionally shared with Rail; rail slopes remain unverified. At rest, maximum traction is used. Each 0.2 s increment evaluates acceleration at its initial speed, then updates speed and advances using the average of the initial and final speeds.

    Effective traction ×2 and constant resistance of 0.02 m/s² are calibrated against synchronized Avelia/TGV speed, distance and overtaking measurements. Catalogue traction retains its original value. The same motion model drives rail Race and passenger/freight Service.

    Optional gravity remains theoretical. Speed-cap behavior and other formations need independent tests. Race excludes terminal braking and stops, but a segment profile anticipates lower track limits. Service adds terminal braking and handling; route profiles retain speed between segments. Equilibrium markers show a 99% approach, not a finite arrival at the exact asymptote.

    From a video race to measured standing starts.

    Calculation & assumptions

    How is this calculated?

    For the selected distance and utilization, we calculate each train’s A–B–A transport throughput, then divide the fleet’s yearly running costs by the number of transported units it can deliver in one game year. Lower is better. Values are absolute: selecting another train does not change them.

    Running cost per capacity = yearly running costs ÷ (transported capacity per second × 1,460)

    The calendar runs at four simulation seconds per day, with 365 days per year: 1,460 simulation seconds per game year. A value of $900 means $900 of running costs per completed passenger journey. An outward journey and its return count separately. This uses the game’s yearly vehicle running costs, not a ticket price; purchase and infrastructure costs are excluded. The calculation assumes continuous service and this calendar speed.

    Why exclude ticket revenue?

    We assume the fare depends on the distance between stations and is the same for every train, consistent with our measurements on one route. For the same station pair, revenue is therefore fare × passenger throughput. If we equalize throughput, revenue is identical: maintenance is what differentiates the trains. Without targets the ratio compares proportional fleets. With targets, whole trains and their actual utilization are counted.

    This comparison excludes purchase costs, congestion and the effect of journey duration on demand. It does not compare the total profit of one individual train. Revenue assumptions remain empirical rather than a complete description of the game’s pricing.

    Assumptions & limitations

    Identical trains, evenly spaced, with equal passenger flow in both directions. Without targets, the model uses one train at the selected utilization. With a target flow, utilization becomes an upper limit and a whole fleet is chosen according to the flow and interval policy. Actual utilization and transfer times adjust to the requested demand. At each terminal, all passengers leave before the next group boards. Each leg starts from rest; constant braking is included. Platform penalties, traffic and variable waits for late passengers are excluded.

    Distance is shared with the race chart. An hour means 3,600 simulation seconds.

    Experimental calibration: 1 passenger/s per multiplier unit, in both directions. Total rate = base rate × sum of the contributing vehicle multipliers. Braking defaults to 2.5 m/s²; fixed delays to 6 seconds per terminal (2 before unloading, 2 before loading, 2 before departure). The extracted loading speeds are summed; zero-speed power cars are excluded, and visual sections within one articulated model count once. Empty mass is used; passenger load showed no visible acceleration effect.

    This model assumes enough passengers to sustain the chosen utilization, regardless of journey time. Very long journeys can reduce demand in the game. The Handcar’s very low maintenance can therefore make it the theoretical winner here, while its slow service may attract too few passengers on long routes. Demand is not modelled, so this ranking is not a complete recommendation for a real line. Deselect the Handcar to compare the other trains.

    Infrastructure and compositions

    Rail service uses the lower of the vehicle and infrastructure speed limits (100, 160 or 350 km/h). Coupled identical multiple units add capacity, length, loading speed and running costs proportionally; mass, power and traction also scale together, so acceleration is unchanged. Coupling is considered only with both a target rate and a target frequency. Choose the closest achievable frequency at one-second display precision, then the lowest fleet running costs. The At most policy remains a hard maximum interval. A platform length limit excludes overlong trains entirely; no loading penalty is modelled. Coupling limits and proportional performance remain assumptions.

    Service targets

    Our target rate is passenger journeys per game year (1,460 simulation seconds) per direction, with the same target in both directions. Frequency is expressed as the interval between trains, as in the game. At most chooses enough whole trains to respect a maximum interval at each station. Closest to target chooses the achievable interval nearest to the requested one, while still serving the required passenger flow; its interval can exceed the target. Enabling only frequency assumes enough demand to maintain the selected utilization. Station congestion, bunching and platform capacity are not modelled. With a fixed flow, maintenance cost includes spare capacity in the chosen fleet; integer train counts can create steps in cost curves.

    Understanding the results

    A stop includes unloading, loading and fixed delays. Travel includes braking to rest. Both directions count as passenger journeys; utilization can represent an average. Maintenance per journey divides the game’s maintenance cost by journeys provided in 1,460 simulation seconds. Lower values need less maintenance for equal throughput. It excludes fares and purchase costs.

    Reading the graphs

    The distance axis ends at your selected route distance, shown by the vertical marker. Hover a curve to identify its train. Log scale crops values below the displayed minimum.

    Phase focus expands leadership phases and compresses the final phase. Marks on the distance axis show scale changes. The displayed window starts at the lesser of 100 m and one tenth of your selected route; no claim is made beyond that distance. A single leadership phase keeps a uniform scale.

    All crossovers gives equal space between every ranking change, including changes among non-leading trains. The search begins at the lesser of 100 m and one tenth of your selected route. The chart ends at the selected route distance.

    Compare your saved railway freight compositions on a two-stop A–B–A line. Each leg starts and ends at rest; acceleration, braking and the track speed limit are included. Empty mass is used in both directions: the loaded/empty vegetable test showed no visible difference, but other cargo has not been independently tested.

    Freight handling is 0.0625 cargo units/s per aggregate multiplier. A terminal and a warehouse each double handling at their own stop. Pauses are 2 seconds before each active transfer and 2 seconds before departure: 4 seconds per stop for an empty return, 6 for a loaded return. A loaded return assumes the same utilization in both directions; deliveries count both legs. Transfers are sequential.

    Target rate is total cargo units delivered per game year (1,460 simulation seconds). Whole trains satisfy the requested rate and interval policy; utilization becomes a ceiling. Compositions retain their chosen vehicles and are never automatically coupled. Traffic, queues, mixed cargo, purchase costs, infrastructure costs and revenue are excluded. Numeric fleet changes and crossovers are sampled.

    Road & tram service model

    Calculation & limitations

    Compare trucks on A–B–A. Fixed freight handling factor: 0.0625 (1/16).

    Cycle = travel A→B + travel B→A + loading + unloading + 2 × terminal delay
    Cost / capacity = yearly running costs × cycle / (1,460 × capacity transported per cycle)

    Uses 4 simulation seconds/day and 365 days/year. Experimental road motion uses the rail traction-to-power calculation: 0.2 s increments, twice the captured traction, 0.02 m/s² resistance. The shared grade-force multiplier is 0.38, fitted to MAN runs at displayed 10% and 20% grades and provisionally extended to Rail. Road braking is omitted: the empty MAN stopped naturally from 80 km/h in 3.6 s, whereas the empty FAW Jiefang J6P stopped from 100 km/h in 10 s. These checks do not establish a common deceleration. Each leg accelerates from rest; lower segment limits apply instantly, and no braking time or distance is added at terminals. Initial MAN flat-road acceleration and displayed 10%/20% uphill checks support the provisional acceleration and grade choices. Other vehicles and grades need validation. Both directions use empty mass; congestion and cargo weight are excluded. No queues, intermediate stops or purchase costs. Handling factors are fixed at 0.0625 for freight and 1 for passengers, with 6 s of pauses per terminal for loading plus unloading, or 4 s for a single transfer operation. Freight timings are approximately supported by MAN 19.304 measurements; bus and tram terminal timings need independent validation.

    Long-haul limit: ranking approaches running costs ÷ (capacity × effective speed)

    The supplied datasets cover all installed non-campaign road vehicles and trams: 25 buses (1892–2029), 21 passenger trams (1890–2024), 9 freight trams (1890–2030) and 52 freight road vehicles: 12 general-purpose, 10 bulk tippers, 11 goods box trucks, 8 flatbeds and 11 liquid vehicles, including horse-drawn wagons. Generic French names are translated into English; model names are retained. The year filter includes all vehicles introduced by the selected year. Retirement dates are not applied.
