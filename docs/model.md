# Model, data and chart conventions

## Motion model

Each train starts at rest, on level track by default. Rail acceleration is `min(2 × Fmax, P/v) / m − 0.02` in SI units. Maximum force is used at rest to avoid division by zero. The effective traction multiplier changes motion only; displayed catalogue traction remains the original force.

Each 0.2-second increment evaluates acceleration from its initial speed, updates speed, and advances by the average of the initial and final speeds times 0.2. Uncapped constant-traction increments are evaluated in bulk using their exact quadratic trajectory. Power-phase increments are computed once per model and reused. Between increments, speed is linear and distance quadratic; table lookup and a stable quadratic inverse keep speed/distance, service and crossover queries fast and mutually consistent. The trajectory retains full increments even if a phase threshold falls within one. The transition marker identifies the first increment boundary at which the engine switches to power-limited acceleration. Nominal speed caps clamp the final speed of an increment; this boundary behavior still requires a dedicated game test.

Flat-track calibration uses synchronized standard Avelia Liberty and TGV Duplex: eight rounded speeds at 16/32/64/128 increments match, and four reported nose displacements at 16/128 increments agree within 0.17 m when measured from each train's own initial nose position. A separate 5001.46 m checkpoint reproduces both rounded speeds (247 and 205 km/h). The noses have a repeatable initial offset of 0.47 m; which train starts ahead is not yet specified. Visual lead equals initial nose lead plus the difference in travelled distances; the approximately 768 m visual separation and earlier nose crossover observations therefore need that correction before quantitative validation. The model assumes identical initial positions and predicts the first Avelia/TGV crossing at about 234 m. A later increment-resolved run supersedes the initial approximate 170 m observation: TGV is last ahead at increment 83 and Avelia first ahead at 84, with reported Avelia distance 240.22 m. The model gives 240.59 m at increment 84 (0.37 m residual) and reproduces that crossover bracket if the TGV nose initially leads by 0.47 m; this direction is an inference rather than separately confirmed evidence. Measurements and their limitations are retained in `data/experiments.json`. All 117 extracted rail model records specify `friction = 0.02`; interpreting this as constant resistance matches these runs. Corrected positional measurements, other formations, or speed-cap tests would justify revisiting the calibration. This is an observed behavioral model, not proof of the game's internal integrator.

SI conversions: 1 kgf = 9.80665 N; 1 metric horsepower (ch) = 735.5 W. The picker and Composition show power in kW, converted from `powerCh`, and traction in N, converted from the captured `tractionKgf`. Source fields retain their original units for provenance; the physics already uses newtons internally. All charts identify every selected train, including the full catalogue. Wide charts use curve-end names; mobile charts use colored legends to preserve plot space, with arrival labels also kept above the distance/time chart. Hovering changes emphasis rather than hiding names. In Race motion charts, hovering or focusing a train adds traction→power, braking and segment-transition points with horizontal/vertical coordinates and guide lines. Uniform-reference motion retains its traction→power and power→speed-limit milestones. Traction-only caps have a single marker. Power-limited uphill curves use a clearly labelled 99% equilibrium milestone instead of claiming an exact finite transition at the asymptote. Labels avoid every visible transition marker with an 8 px clearance, including after edge clamping, and avoid other labels. Markers are painted above the labels. Markers outside the visible domain, including logarithmic floors, are omitted. Exact transition abscissas join the curve samples so the points lie on the drawn curve. Highlighting only updates a small overlay and does not recalculate or replace the curves. Charts and readouts use minutes:seconds, rounded to the nearest second; the model and CSV use seconds.

The underlying uniform motion model includes calibrated constant resistance and excludes additional aerodynamic drag, terminal braking, stops and loading. The shared rail route adds braking for upcoming lower speed limits; service analysis adds braking to rest and terminal handling. Grades add gravity as described below. A few matching timings do not establish the game's full physics. Repeatable observations with accurately known distances would justify refining these assumptions.

## Constant route gradient (theoretical)

The shared route allows −20% to +20% grades for Rail and Road, in 0.1 percentage-point increments with a zero default. One segment is the constant-gradient case; longer profiles apply the same gravity rule segment by segment. Grade is rise divided by horizontal run: `theta = atan(gradePercent / 100)`. Positive is uphill A→B. Distance remains travelled distance along the route. Race uses one outbound leg; services reverse segment order and grade signs on the return. Empty vehicle mass is used on both legs, without payload mass. This extension is **theoretical, not calibrated against the game**, and the control help says so. Revisit it after repeatable paired measurements with known grades, lengths and compositions.

Rail increments use acceleration `min(2 × Fmax, P/v) / m − 0.02 − g sin(theta)`, with `g = 9.80665 m/s²`. A train unable to overcome uphill gravity from rest is excluded from service calculations and all service phase cohorts, retaining its selection. In Race, a downhill-only run can still start. The same calibrated increment model applies at zero and nonzero grade, avoiding a discontinuity around flat track. Uphill equilibrium is `P / (m × (0.02 + g sin(theta)))` when the combined resistance is positive; the final relative speed gap of 1e−8 is approximated by a steady tail. Monotone motion tables make asymptotic inversions a binary search rather than repeated numerical integration. Vehicle and infrastructure caps still apply. Service braking uses `2.5 + g sin(theta)` m/s²: uphill gravity assists, downhill gravity opposes; nonpositive net braking is rejected. A–B–A motion caches are keyed by each directional model and braking value. Calculation details display travel A→B / B→A separately.

## Shared route profiles (theoretical)

Race and both Rail/Road Service use the same ordered A→B list of one to 24 segments. The Design → Route view holds the editor and a symbolic elevation sketch; edits update that shared route immediately. A single segment describes constant grade and speed limit. The sketch offers equal-width segments or a linear distance axis. Its vertical scale remains exaggerated; it is not surveyed terrain. Selecting a segment opens its fields immediately below the preview without moving keyboard focus; the segment range and symbolic-scale note follow the fields. The selected band has + buttons to insert before or after it, arrows to move it and a trash icon to remove it. The right sidebar summarizes all segments and selects short ones; add-after and remove appear on the selected row, with add-at-end below the list. The editor accepts 0.01–100 km per segment, −20% to +20% grade, 10–350 km/h speed limits, and at most 100 km total. The final segment cannot be removed. One-way distance is the sum of segment lengths. Returning B→A reverses the list and negates each grade without changing track limits. No intermediate stop is implied. Race starts at rest and ends at B without a terminal stop. Rail passenger/freight Service starts and ends at rest on both legs; Road retains its steady-speed model. Payload mass is still excluded.

The rail profile integrator uses the calibrated 0.2 s traction/power increments and average-velocity distance. At segment boundaries speed is continuous. An advance braking envelope includes every upcoming lower limit and, for services, the B terminus. Its conservative braking rate is the minimum of `2.5 + g sin(theta)` across the intervening segments; a fractional traction/braking step meets the envelope without an instantaneous speed change. A stopped train unable to resume is excluded. This braking controller, variable grades and segment-limit handling are theoretical and need controlled game measurements. A single uniform segment agrees with the existing Race/service model to sub-millisecond numerical precision in regression tests.

Each train's Race time-domain curve ends at its own arrival at B, without a horizontal tail after arrival; profile crossovers are not extrapolated beyond that finite route. Service distance sweeps scale every segment length proportionally while retaining gradients and limits, so the horizontal axis is a family of similarly shaped routes, not a claim about newly inserted track. The profile crossover search is sampled before numerical root refinement and could miss a very narrow phase; the UI marks it as sampled. Revisit the profile model after repeatable observations on routes with known segment geometry and limit/braking transitions.

Road uses steady speed separately for every shared route segment. Uphill speed is the lower of the vehicle/segment speed cap and `P / (m g sin(theta))`; the starting traction check applies on each uphill segment. Downhill speed remains capped. Outbound travel is `sum(segmentDistanceKm / segmentSpeedKmh × 3600)`; the return reverses segment order and grade signs. Acceleration and braking remain excluded, so short-route timings remain optimistic. The harmonic mean of the resulting directional speeds supplies round-trip long-haul and phase coefficients; the readout shows each leg separately. Missing mechanical data excludes a vehicle only where a nonzero grade needs it. Blocked vehicles never enter cost/rank phase comparisons. Both legs use identical empty mass; reversing the route swaps directional travel times while leaving total service cost unchanged.

## Distance and speed views

Route length comes from the shared segment sum, bounded at 100 km. The Route editor retains 0.01 km length steps. Distance sweeps preserve segment proportions, gradients and speed limits. For reference, the earlier flat four-train Metroliner/Twindexx/TGV/Fuxing comparison’s last ranking change is about 8.382 km; across all 17 captured trains it is about 82.836 km.

Distance over time ends when the last selected train reaches B; its vertical ceiling is 110% of the route distance. Time over distance ends at B. Race readout sorts individual arrival times, with speed at arrival. Profile curves use each train’s continuous segment trajectory; they stop at its arrival instead of extending beyond the configured route.

The speed chart switches between **Speed over time** and **Speed over distance**, using the shared route trajectory. Each train’s time-domain curve ends on arrival at B; the distance domain ends at the shared route length. Upcoming lower segment limits can cause braking; Race excludes terminal braking. Each axis retains linear/log controls. CSV and SVG exports are available for speed and distance/time charts. Speed/distance exports sample distance and retain the corresponding time, distance and speed columns. CSV includes the full sampled domain from zero; SVG reflects the selected axes and includes a legend. Uniform composition references and calibration checks retain their own motion-domain horizons independently of this route-limited comparison.

Logarithmic axes crop small values: time starts at 10 s, distance at 0.1 km and speed at 10 km/h. For very small domains the floor becomes one tenth of the maximum. Zero stays in CSV but has no logarithmic position. Axis reversal preserves each quantity's scale choice.

## Crossover experiments

Crossings during acceleration are located numerically with mixed linear/log samples and bisection. A pair is skipped only when equal grade, no lower speed cap, and no lower traction or power per mass prove that one train can never fall behind the other. Immutable model pairs cache their roots across selections and diagrams; model replacement, speed-cap changes or a new timing function invalidate the pair. The route-slider limit and both phase diagrams share a single computed story instead of searching the same pairs three times. The steady-speed tail uses analytical intersections. Very close crossings or almost-identical trains would justify a fully analytical solver before claiming a guaranteed exact cutoff.

**Arrival curves around crossovers** follows the leader envelope, not crossings among slower trains. Its window ends 15% beyond the last leader-change distance. If there is no leader change, it covers the leaders' acceleration to 105% of their speed-cap distance, at least 1 km. Distance over time puts the winner at the top; reversing the axes puts it at the bottom. Winning segments can be emphasized while others fade. Here, “best” means fastest, not cheapest or most comfortable.

Phase focus axes are continuous piecewise-linear transforms based on leader-change distances and arrival times. Intermediate intervals get equal space; the final stable tail gets 30% of a normal interval. These axes can create visual bends where a curve crosses a scale boundary. Orange grid lines and axis marks identify real scale changes; a single phase has none. Linear axes remove these scale effects. The display keeps real units and sparse vertical labels.

**Rank crossovers** starts at the lesser of 100 m and one tenth of the selected route, discarding earlier crossings from the display without altering ranks after that point. Linear uses a uniform distance scale. All crossovers assigns equally spaced positions to all distinct ranking changes, including non-leaders; simultaneous crossings share a position. The final stable tail is compressed. The ranking domain includes later crossings that do not extend the arrival-curves domain.

The shared table below the crossover views gives leadership ranges and arrival time at each range's start. Infinity means the last leader/ranking persists in this model; the plotted tail itself is finite. No Pareto frontier or network-wide economic optimization is implemented.

## Experimental A–B–A line capacity

Line capacity uses the active selection and shared one-way distance, including the Handcar when selected. Its unusually low maintenance can dominate the theoretical efficiency metric. The model assumes enough demand to sustain the selected occupancy regardless of journey duration; it does not model how longer trips reduce attractiveness and demand. In particular, slow Handcar service on long routes may attract too few passengers to achieve the assumed throughput. This ranking is conditional on occupancy, not a complete line recommendation. Users can deselect the Handcar for a comparison of other trains. Without service targets, one empty-mass train runs continuously with equal average occupancy in both directions, unlimited demand and a suitable platform. Optional flow and interval targets dimension a fleet as described below. Each leg starts and ends at rest. Fares, purchase costs, traffic and variable waits for late passengers are excluded. Race and crossover charts remain standing-start comparisons without terminal braking or stops; braking for lower segment speed limits is included.

Let `N = passengerCapacity × occupancy`, `S = loadingUnloadingSpeedMultiplier`, `C = carCount`, and `r = baseRate × S × C`. The legacy fallback assumes every counted car contributes the same displayed multiplier; all current rail records instead use an explicit extracted handling sum. Formation counts supplied by Flavien cover the original 16 catalogue trains. An explicit `formationLoadingUnloadingSpeedMultiplier` takes precedence over `S × C`; the sum is recorded for all 17 rail vehicles. Avelia Liberty has 11 elements, including two power cars with zero loading speed and nine passenger cars at 5×; its aggregate multiplier is 45, not 55. TEE VT 11.5 uses 30 rather than 42, Inter-City 125 uses 24 rather than 30, Re 450 uses 9 rather than 12, and ICE 1 uses 36 rather than 44. Their zero-loading-speed power cars do not contribute. Talent 1 is one articulated model with multiplier 4, rather than three independent model instances at 4 (12). Head cars that do have positive passenger loading speed, as in the TGV, remain included. Avelia capacity, physical totals and normal-scale cost sums agree with the detailed screenshot. The extracted handling sum remains subject to the same in-game transfer assumptions as the rest of this model. The original four-car loading experiment used a locomotive and wagons, not the Autorail Uerdingen.

At each terminal everyone unloads, then the next group boards. Both transfers use the same base rate, default **1 passenger/s per multiplier unit**. Metroliner measurements with two cars at 1.5× yielded 38 passengers in approximately 12.45 seconds in continuous simulation, close to the predicted 12.67 seconds. A Roter Pfeil with one car at 5× transferred 19 additional passengers in 19 steps (3.8 seconds). Observed occasional waits for the last passengers are not included in this ideal throughput.

Fixed terminal delays default to **6 seconds**: 2 before unloading, 2 before loading and 2 before departure. Observed departure delays around 2–2.5 seconds remain an approximate calibration rather than a precise universal claim. Extracted component sums and these delay assumptions should be revisited if repeatable measurements of other formations disagree.

Constant braking defaults to **2.5 m/s²**. A simulation step was calibrated at 0.2 seconds by 200 steps over 1 km at constant 90 km/h. Braking took 50 steps from 90 km/h; the Metroliner took 87 steps from an observed 158 km/h, consistent with this deceleration. To preserve route distance, the analysis solves for the motion-model time `t` such that `distance(t) + speed(t)²/(2 × deceleration) = route distance`. Leg duration is `t + speed(t)/deceleration`. Short routes brake before top speed; the whole braking duration is not simply added to the original race time. The shared infrastructure cap is 100, 160 or 350 km/h.

```text
terminalSeconds = 2 × N/r + fixedDelays
roundTripSeconds = travelAIncludingBraking + travelBIncludingBraking + 2 × terminalSeconds
journeysPerSecond = 2 × N / roundTripSeconds
journeysPerHour = journeysPerSecond × 3600
maintenancePerThroughput = annualMaintenance / journeysPerHour
transportPerMaintenance = journeysPerHour / annualMaintenance
maintenancePerJourney = annualMaintenance / (journeysPerSecond × 1460)
efficiency = journeysPerSecond / annualMaintenance  # internal ranking score
```

Both directions count as journeys, not unique passengers. An hour is 3,600 simulation seconds. The displayed absolute value is `annualMaintenance / (journeysPerSecond × 1460)`, in maintenance dollars per completed passenger journey; lower is better. Flavien supplied the calendar conversion: four simulation seconds per day and 365 days per game year, or 1,460 seconds. This assumes continuous operation at that calendar speed. An outward journey and return are counted separately. The value does not depend on selected competitors and excludes purchase cost and fares. The hourly ratios remain available in the model; the internal ranking score remains `journeysPerSecond / annualMaintenance`, giving the same ordering. The service readout reports passenger journeys per game year and direction, using the same 1,460-second conversion. Zero occupancy gives no best choice. Costs use normal difficulty and one continuously operated train.

A visual drag race between identical trains carrying 60 and two passengers showed no observable acceleration difference; passenger mass is omitted. Reproducible timing differences would justify revisiting this choice. The occupancy slider ranges from 1% to 100%, keeping all economic views visible when reducing occupancy. Distance and occupancy controls are local to the page; distance uses a slider from 0.01 to 30 km, extending to accommodate longer distances entered in its adjacent number field or in the race section. Calibration parameters are fixed model defaults, not editable page controls.

The method introduction explains the ratio before the controls, with a numerical example and a subsection on revenue exclusion. On the same station pair, equalizing throughput across proportional fleets makes revenue identical if fares per passenger are identical. Measurements on one route support this fare assumption, but do not establish the full game pricing formula. The comparison excludes purchase cost, congestion and endogenous demand changes; it does not claim to rank the profit of individual trains.

### Economic crossover views

Economic crossovers compare the same throughput-per-maintenance score. All pairwise ranking changes from the lesser of **0.1 km and one tenth of the selected distance** to the selected route distance are searched on a combined 512-interval linear/logarithmic grid, with bisection of sign changes and deduplication of coincident roots. Persistent ties retain shared ranks; zero occupancy and empty selections have no meaningful crossover story. This numerical search may miss very closely spaced crossings without a sign change on the sampling grid; an analytical solver would be justified if repeatable examples expose that limitation.

The efficiency crossover curves merge ranking intervals with the same leaders into leadership phases. Phase focus gives each phase equal space and compresses the last to 30% when several exist. A single phase remains uniform. Winning curve segments are emphasized; other segments fade. Linear distance and linear/log maintenance-cost controls are independent of the standard efficiency chart. Marks at phase boundaries identify distance scale changes.

The economic rank chart includes crossings among non-leading trains. Its distance modes are Linear and All crossovers; the latter gives equal space to every interval between ranking changes. Closely packed tick labels are thinned to preserve readability. Both views end at the selected distance, without implying the final ranking holds at infinity. One shared table lists leadership phases. Selection, occupancy, distance and hover stay synchronized with the service readout and picker. Motion and economic rankings remain separate.


## Annual flow and station interval targets

Both targets are optional and disabled initially. Flow is completed passenger journeys per **game year per direction**; convert it to per-second flow with `D = annualDirectionalDemand / 1460`. The occupancy slider becomes an upper limit when flow is fixed. Identical trains are assumed evenly spaced and unimpeded at stations; platforms, traffic and bunching are excluded.

Let `C0 = travelASeconds + travelBSeconds + 2 × fixedTerminalDelay`, `r` be the per-train transfer rate, `Pmax` the capacity at the occupancy limit, and `n` the number of trains. A train carries `P = D × C0 / (n − 4D/r)` passengers per leg. This solves the flow and loading-time equations together, rather than assuming full trains after scaling fleet size. Capacity requires `n ≥ D × (C0 + 4Pmax/r) / Pmax`. An interval no larger than `h` additionally requires `n ≥ 4D/r + C0/h`. Choose the smallest feasible whole count, then recompute handling times and actual occupancy.

The **Closest to target** policy compares the adjacent whole counts around the ideal interval count, bounded by the minimum count needed for capacity. It minimizes absolute interval error, breaking ties toward fewer trains; the obtained interval can exceed the target. With no flow target, occupancy stays fixed and frequency alone scales capacity and maintenance together, leaving cost per journey unchanged.

With a fixed flow, fleet maintenance is divided by `2 × annualDirectionalDemand`. Spare capacity still costs maintenance, so tighter intervals may make a fleet less economical. The readout includes fleet size, actual interval, actual occupancy, annual directional flow and fleet maintenance. Service targets propagate to all economic graphs and their caches; they do not affect Race motion. Fixed-flow cost curves are sampled staircases because whole fleet counts create jumps. The numerical crossover search can miss very narrow ranking intervals between densely spaced fleet changes; a repeatable missed interval would justify enumerating every fleet-count boundary.

### Infrastructure and coupled multiple units

Rail economic motion uses the minimum of the vehicle maximum speed and each shared route segment’s speed limit before calculating acceleration and braking. Segment limits apply to Race speed/time, arrival and phase diagrams too. The Route editor offers 100, 160 and 350 km/h presets plus a numeric limit. Catalogue source values remain unchanged. A single segment supplies the composition reference cap; multi-segment routes retain the last uniform reference.

Coupling evaluates identical passenger units only when both rate and frequency targets are active. A formation of `k` units multiplies capacity, car count, loading/unloading rate, length and annual vehicle running costs by `k`. Mass, traction and power would scale by the same factor, so the acceleration model is unchanged. A single formation still incurs the same fixed terminal delay. Coupling limits and proportional performance remain assumptions; revisit them if a tested formation cannot couple or its handling differs.

For coupled formations, frequency is the primary objective, then fleet running costs, then the shorter formation. Compare intervals at the readout’s one-second precision, rounding the actual interval and target before comparing their difference. The maximum policy still enforces the actual unrounded interval no larger than the target; closest mode can exceed it. Without coupling the existing individual-unit frequency policy is retained.

Search fleet sizes and solve candidate formation sizes analytically using `h = C0 / (n − A/k)`, where `A = 4D/r` for one unit. Capacity requires `k ≥ (D × C0/Pmax + A)/n`. For fixed fleet size the interval decreases as units are added; consider the capacity minimum, formations around the requested interval, and the cheapest formation within an equivalent rounded-second interval. With no platform limit, the interval tends to `C0/n`; rounding to seconds gives a finite least-cost representative of this limit instead of demanding infinitely many units. This replaces the former bound on units by single-unit fleet size, which was justified only for cost-first optimization. Fleets larger than the single-unit baseline (plus the adjacent count for rounding) cannot improve its interval error: additional units only lower their already-shorter intervals.

Without a rate target, coupling adds costs without improving the cycle or interval, so a single unit remains selected. Annual fleet costs count both formations and units per formation. Without a platform bound some frequency-first compositions can be extremely long; use the length limit to constrain physically usable results.

An optional positive platform length is a hard upper bound on formation length. ### Truck route calculator with handling

The calculator is the only truck results view; the former long-haul proxy table is removed. Assume one truck on A–B–A, steady-speed travel and utilization `u` (1–100%). Default to loaded outbound and empty return; optionally carry equal loads in both directions. Per-leg load is `capacity × u`, while cargo delivered per cycle is that load times one or two. Each delivered unit is loaded and unloaded exactly once. Return travel always counts, including empty returns.

For each direction, `travelSeconds = sum(segmentDistanceKm / segmentSpeedKmh × 3600)`. The equivalent mean leg duration is `distanceKm / effectiveSpeedKmh × 3600`, with the harmonic directional speed. `rate = loadSpeedFactor × loadingUnloadingSpeedMultiplier × facilityMultiplier`; `loadingSeconds = unloadingSeconds = deliveredPerCycle / rate` when both terminals have the same multiplier. `cycleSeconds = outboundTravelSeconds + returnTravelSeconds + loadingSeconds + unloadingSeconds + terminalPauseA + terminalPauseB`. Cost per delivered cargo unit is `annualMaintenance × cycleSeconds / (GAME_YEAR_SECONDS × deliveredPerCycle)`, using the same 1,460 simulation seconds/year as Service. ### Heterogeneous terminal handling

`analyseLine` currently uses `rate = baseRate × loadingUnloadingSpeedMultiplier × carCount`. That is a homogeneous-formation assumption and cannot be applied unchanged to locomotive-plus-wagon trains. In particular, a non-carrying locomotive must not increase transfer speed by increasing `carCount`.

The builder preserves component references and quantities and computes `formationLoadingUnloadingSpeedMultiplier = sum(quantity × carrying-component multiplier)`. It exposes `handlingRate` after applying the category factor (`1` passengers / `0.0625` freight), without facility bonuses. Locomotives with no capacity fields add no handling contribution; this is an explicit builder assumption, not a measured zero in the source. Compartments and alternative mixed-cargo configurations are not supported by this first builder; only one compatible freight group is selected. Do not manufacture an average multiplier merely to satisfy the old interface.

Flavien's heterogeneous-wagon test on 2026-10-07 reported A (capacity 18, multiplier 8): 36 s; B (capacity 8, multiplier 1): 130 s; A + B: 45 s. At freight factor 0.0625, summed-rate predictions are 36 s, 128 s and 46.22 s. This supports using a shared aggregate rate for the tested same-cargo composition, rather than waiting for an independent slow wagon or summing wagon durations. Exact timing boundaries, wagon identities, terminal settings and repetitions were not supplied with this result; it does not establish mixed-commodity behaviour.

`analyseLine` now prefers an explicit `formationLoadingUnloadingSpeedMultiplier` when present and otherwise keeps the legacy `per-car multiplier × carCount` path. Thus existing homogeneous train calculations remain unchanged. `analyseService` preserves that aggregate field and respects `automaticCouplingAllowed: false` on custom compositions; fleet copies still multiply costs normally. The builder sets this flag, reports missing fields and exposes `model: null` when physical data is incomplete. Unknown costs do not prevent a mechanical preview but disable `serviceReady`. Custom freight inputs must not be passed through the passenger service adapter.

Terminal pauses depend on active operations: 2 s before each transfer operation plus 2 s before departure. Unload plus load gives 6 s; one operation gives 4 s. An empty freight return means one loading-only stop and one unloading-only stop, not two unload-plus-load stops. The freight cycle applies the validated category factor and this operation accounting independently at both stops.

### Exploring service parameters and catalogue years

Service phase and rank diagrams vary distance, catalogue year (1900–2035), utilization (1–100%), target transport rate or target interval. Passenger rate is per direction; freight rate is total delivered cargo per game year. Other scenario settings stay fixed. Rate and interval axes apply that target to the diagrams even when the corresponding service checkbox is off; they do not modify the scenario controls. Rate is an assumed input, not a prediction from speed, frequency or passenger satisfaction. Utilization is a ceiling when a rate is imposed. Current settings are marked only when applicable. Year diagrams include selected future vehicles and make each available at its introduction year; source retirement dates are not applied.

Race can compare arrival times over introduction years for the fixed route distance, using the same phase renderer and shared infrastructure cap. Vertical rank transitions stay neutral and only horizontal winning plateaus are emphasized.

Road service targets reuse railway whole-vehicle fleet sizing, without automatic coupling. Passenger rate is per year per direction; freight rate is total delivered units per year, including a loaded return when enabled. A fixed rate reduces actual utilization below its ceiling as needed. Frequency uses maximum or closest interval policies. Readouts include fleet size, actual utilization, interval, total annual delivery and annual fleet maintenance. Without targets the existing single-vehicle results are unchanged. Target-aware phase costs use the sized fleet at each abscissa. Numeric fleet boundaries remain sampled; a reproducible missed narrow ranking phase would justify solving and enumerating every count boundary. Acceleration, traffic, queues and endogenous demand remain outside the road model.

### Composition drafts and copies

Opening the current draft in Compare enables a session-only comparison before saving it. It does not overwrite a saved definition or create browser persistence. Design → Route links back to Race or Service using the current shared route; the comparison sidebar’s composition action opens Composition. Composition curves remain a single-train uniform-reference analysis with Rate, Frequency, platform and whole-fleet targets disabled. A single shared segment supplies their reference grade; with several segments the saved uniform grade remains the reference. A single segment also supplies the reference speed cap. Selected-route service applies constraints and traverses every shared route segment. This separation supports quick changes to a composition and route; it does not introduce automatic composition search.

Duplicate creates an independent unsaved composition with a fresh ID and copied component quantities. Save commits the copy to browser storage; editing it never changes the source definition. Changing catalogue carrier, category, cargo or year only changes the vehicles being browsed. The draft keeps its own carrier/category/cargo context, name and quantities, displayed above the composition. Incompatible additions are refused; New composition explicitly starts a draft in the selected catalogue context. Browsing earlier years does not make an existing draft invalid.

## Railway Freight Service

Service switches between Passengers and Freight without adding an analysis tab. Freight compares custom rail compositions with positive compatible cargo capacity. All freight means a general-purpose composition; a specific cargo group also accepts general-purpose trains. Standalone engines and wagons are never treated as complete services. Without a compatible recipe, the result panels and their charts are hidden and replaced by an invitation to configure a train. Year, selection and length exclusions offer the corresponding action; selected future trains still participate in year phase diagrams.

`analyseEconomicService` dispatches to the passenger service or `analyseFreightService`, exposing `maintenancePerUnit` to the same distance, phase and rank renderers. The freight adapter reuses the exact passenger stop-to-stop acceleration/cruising/braking calculation, including the infrastructure cap, and uses empty mass in both directions. Configured formations remain intact: fleet copies add costs and throughput, without automatic coupling.

For capacity `C`, utilization `f`, aggregate multiplier `M` and loaded-return flag `r` (0 or 1), cargo per loaded leg is `q = C f`. Handling rates are `a = 0.0625 M × bonuses at A` and `b = 0.0625 M × bonuses at B`. Terminal and warehouse each contribute ×2 only at their own stop. Stop times are `(1+r) q/a + pause` at A and `(1+r) q/b + pause` at B; pause is 4 seconds for one active transfer, 6 for unloading plus loading. Cycle time is the sum of outbound and return stop-to-stop travel times plus both stops. Each cycle delivers `(1+r) q` units. Loaded return assumes identical loads in both directions; each delivery counts once.

An optional total annual delivery target and maximum/closest interval policy reuse the whole-fleet solver. A fixed rate reduces actual utilization below its ceiling as necessary; cycle time is recalculated for the actual load. Annual maintenance is multiplied by train count and divided by deliveries in 1,460 simulation seconds. Without targets, one configured train runs at the chosen utilization. Readouts distinguish delivery count, fleet cost, handling rates at A/B, station times, travel and cycle duration. Purchase, infrastructure, revenue, traffic, queues and mixed cargo remain outside this model. The numeric phase sampler retains the existing narrow-fleet-boundary limitation; reproducible missed phases would justify enumerating count boundaries.
